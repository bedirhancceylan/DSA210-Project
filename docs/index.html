I.Motivation

The intuition was cheap. The evidence was missing.

Working as Founding Chief of Staff at Sollen, an early-stage startup building an occupational health and safety platform, I had watched the same kinds of tasks slip behind schedule for months. Some categories ran late more often than others. Certain developers seemed to absorb delays in a way no spreadsheet had ever made visible. None of it had been formally tested.

This project applies the full data science pipeline taught in DSA 210 — exploratory analysis, hypothesis testing, feature engineering, supervised learning — to a real operational dataset. Three concrete questions structure the investigation:

1. Is task duration meaningfully different across departments?
2. Is task category — by itself — sufficient to predict whether a task will be delayed?
3. Can a feature-engineered model identify the conditions at task creation that most predict eventual delay?

II.Data Source

One team. Eighteen months. Four hundred and eighty-six closed tasks.

The dataset was exported from the Azure DevOps work item history of Sollen's primary product repository, covering every task created and closed between 18 October 2024 and 7 April 2026 — a continuous 18-month window of active development. Tasks still open at extraction time were excluded; cancelled and duplicate items were removed. Developer names were replaced with anonymous labels (Developer_A through Developer_N) before analysis.

Target construction

A task is labelled delayed if its actual duration exceeds 1.5× the median duration for its category. This category-relative threshold is robust to the long tail of the distribution (some tasks ran for over 300 days) and accounts for genuinely different baseline durations across departments — a 14-day design task is normal; a 14-day research task is unusually long.

III.Hypothesis Testing

Three tests. One surprise.

Each test was chosen for a specific structural reason in the data — heavy right-skew ruled out parametric tests; the binary outcome and categorical predictor pointed to chi-square; the ranked nature of duration data pointed to Mann-Whitney for the two-group comparison.

Test 1 · Kruskal-Wallis
Does task duration differ across the five categories?
H = 39.97
p < 0.0001
Reject H₀ — categories differ
Test 2 · Chi-Square
Is task category associated with delay status?
χ² = 5.13, df = 4
p = 0.275
Fail to reject — no association
Test 3 · Mann-Whitney U
Do delayed tasks have longer durations than on-time?
U = 50,252, r = 0.876
p < 0.0001
Reject H₀ — large effect
The chi-square negative result is the most analytically important finding in the study. It told us category alone is not predictive — the real signal had to come from interactions between features. This single insight reshaped the entire ML phase.

IV.Feature Engineering

The hardest part was what to leave out.

Because the target was constructed from actual_duration_days, including duration as a feature would have given the model perfect knowledge of the answer. Three columns were dropped for direct leakage: actual_duration_days, completion_date, and delay_label itself. Two more were dropped for zero variance — priority_level (99.2% one value) and has_urgent_tag (99.4% zero) — reflecting a team that simply isn't using those workflow fields.

The remaining ten engineered features describe only what was knowable at the moment a task was created: who was assigned, what category, what month, what day of week, how loaded that developer already was, how loaded the team was overall, and how that developer's prior tasks had behaved. The two historical features were computed with strict temporal ordering — for each task, only earlier tasks were used to compute the developer's prior performance.

Five models, one winner.

VI.Limitations

What this study cannot tell you.

Six honest caveats that bound the conclusions:

Sample size

486 tasks is small for ML. The wider confidence intervals on per-fold metrics — F1 std of ±0.045 on a mean of 0.517 — reflect this. Doubling the dataset would meaningfully tighten estimates.

Single-team scope

All data comes from one team in one company. The strong importance of creation_month_num may reflect that specific team's operating rhythm and would not necessarily generalize.

The threshold is conventional, not validated

The 1.5× category-median definition of "delayed" is a reasonable choice but is not validated against any external business definition.

Missing context

Story points, dependencies, vacation overlap, external blockers — none captured in the Azure DevOps export. Including these would likely raise the ceiling of model performance.

Conservative model

At the default 0.5 threshold, the model has higher specificity (0.83) than sensitivity (0.50) for delay. A planning tool would benefit from a lower threshold.

Interpretability vs. accuracy

Random Forest is harder to explain to a non-technical stakeholder than logistic regression. For deployment as a team-facing planning tool, a transparent worse model may be preferable.

VII.Future Work

The next seven moves.

Threshold tuning. Precision-recall analysis with a domain cost matrix to identify a more useful operating point than the default 0.5.

Hyperparameter tuning. Grid search over n_estimators, max_depth, min_samples_leaf with stratified CV.

Time-series cross-validation. The current shuffled CV does not respect temporal order. Switching to TimeSeriesSplit would give a more honest estimate of forward-looking performance.

Richer features. Sprint identifiers, parent/child task links, story points, national holidays, team-velocity rolling averages.

Multi-target prediction. Extend from binary classification to duration regression with the same engineered features.

Deployment. Lightweight web interface where the team enters category, assignee, current load — and receives a delay probability.

Benchmarking. Before any deployment, compare the model to human intuition and to a trivial heuristic (assignee's prior delay rate alone). If the gap is small, the model isn't worth the operational complexity.
